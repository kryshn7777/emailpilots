# COMMON Site Brief — Part 03: Story & Choreography
> Used in: Phase 1 (creative blueprint — primary input for the `scene-director` subagent) and Phase 3 (core experience). Output artifact: `docs/direction/STORYBOARD.md`.

## Creative directive
The website must have a deliberate story. Do NOT build `Hero → Features → Pricing → Testimonials → CTA` — too generic. Instead, a coherent narrative with: **setup → curiosity → discovery → escalation → product revelation → emotional payoff → proof → conversion.**

Think like a film director, a game designer, and an advertising creative director simultaneously. The visitor should feel they are going somewhere; the interaction has a beginning, middle, and end. The site communicates the product without forcing the visitor to read a wall of marketing copy.

## Find a better idea (don't take the first metaphor)
Explore multiple concepts internally. Then choose the one with the strongest combination of: memorability · product relevance · emotional impact · shareability · visual uniqueness · clarity · conversion potential · technical feasibility · mobile performance. The final experience may use 3D, 2D, cinematic transitions, particles, shaders, typography, procedural graphics, video, animation, or a mix — the best medium per scene is your decision.

**Asset feasibility gate:** before any concept or scene is locked into the storyboard, verify that the hero models it needs actually exist as high-quality licensed downloads (Poly Haven first — sourcing rules in part 04). If they don't, redirect the concept or scene to assets that do exist. Direction follows available assets, never the reverse: no fabricated stand-in models.

## The creative blueprint (write BEFORE coding)
Define: **A** core concept · **B** visitor emotional journey · **C** story premise · **D** opening hook · **E** scene sequence · **F** scene transitions · **G** interaction model · **H** product reveal · **I** proof moments · **J** CTA moments · **K** ending · **L** social/shareable moments.

### Per-scene spec (the STORYBOARD.md template — every scene, every field)
scene name · purpose · user emotion · visual composition · camera position · camera movement · objects (each hero model mapped to a sourced, licensed asset — see part 04) · animation · environmental effects · lighting · materials · textures · typography · 3D text · on-screen copy · interaction · scroll behavior · transition in · transition out · sound opportunity (if appropriate) · performance risk · mobile adaptation · accessibility/reduced-motion behavior.

Also define **exact choreography**. Never merely "show a beautiful scene" — specify what actually happens, beat by beat.

## Think in choreography
Scrolling is a storytelling controller, not vertical camera travel. Usable levers: camera movement · object movement · environment transformation · time-of-day changes · lighting transitions · particles · typography entering/exiting · scene morphing · reveal mechanics · scale changes · depth transitions · foreground/background parallax · interaction-triggered events.

Rules: use anticipation and payoff · do not animate everything at once · create visual rhythm · some moments quiet, some spectacular · the visitor should occasionally think "wait, what just happened?" — and then understand why.

## 3D typography
Use 3D text deliberately; no giant floating words just because you can. Every major piece of typography has a purpose. Options: physical 3D lettering embedded in the world · text as part of architecture · text formed from particles · text revealed through camera movement · text becoming objects · text interacting with the environment · typography as navigation through the story. Typography must remain readable; on mobile, simplify or replace difficult 3D text compositions.

## Sound
Do not automatically add music. Evaluate whether sound genuinely improves the experience. If used: optional, respects autoplay restrictions, has controls, supports reduced/no-sound preferences, avoids annoying loops. Subtle sound design can be extremely powerful — but silence may be better.

## Shareability / distribution
Design several "content moments" people will screen-record, post, show friends, discuss, recreate, or build creator content around. Candidates: a spectacular reveal · an unexpected transition · a transformation · a surprising interaction · a visually impossible scene · a memorable final reveal. No gimmicks built solely for social — shareable moments must come naturally from the product story.
